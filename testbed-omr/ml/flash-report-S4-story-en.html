Flash Report · Maritime Bonding Testbed

From a LinUCB Brain to the Storm Test

A short story of how our edge AI was built and stress-tested · 28 October 2026

In one sentence: we gave the two-SIM ship link a small learning brain, then deliberately broke one link to see if the brain would notice and adapt. It did.

1We added a learning brain — LinUCB

Our ship gateway bonds two cellular SIMs. The open question was how much of the camera traffic to send over each SIM when their quality keeps changing. Instead of a fixed rule, we put a small AI at the edge (on the Jetson) that decides this live, every second.

Why LinUCB? The problem is a classic "decide under uncertainty, and keep learning" problem, which is exactly what a contextual bandit solves. LinUCB fits because it is:

  • Contextual — it reads the live state of each SIM (RTT, loss, capacity) and chooses the split from that context, not blindly.
  • Online & lightweight — it learns from each outcome with a simple linear update; no GPU, no offline training, runs comfortably at 1 Hz on the edge.
  • Self-balancing — the "UCB" part makes it occasionally try alternatives (explore) while mostly using what works (exploit), so it keeps adapting instead of getting stuck.
  • Explainable — every decision comes with a score per option and a plain reason, which a full deep-RL policy would not give us.

Reward is simply goodput − 0.05·latency: favour the SIM that moves more data without too much delay.

2Then we hammered it — with tc netem

A brain that is never tested proves nothing. So we built a set of synthetic maritime conditions using Linux tc netem — adding delay, jitter, packet loss and a rate cap to one SIM to mimic a ship sailing from a good port out to a weak cell edge. The profiles (S0–S6) are not arbitrary; they are calibrated to our real PSA Agility drive-test traces.

3The method we ran — Scenario S4 (cell edge)

We degraded one SIM (Simba) while the other (Singtel) stayed healthy, and watched the AI's live decision. S4 applies, on that SIM's uplink:

tc qdisc replace dev wan1 root netem delay 300ms 100ms loss 10% rate 1mbit

= 300 ms delay (±100 ms jitter), 10% packet loss, 1 Mbit/s cap. One subtlety we had to solve: the transport layer (OpenMPTCProuter) also reacts to a bad link and wipes our netem within ≈4 s — so we hold the condition by re-asserting it once per second for the test window, with a mandatory auto-restore afterwards.

4The result — the AI works, then decides

What the AI did

When Simba started dropping packets, the AI shifted the load off it (w → 0, "avoiding the packet-losing SIM"). When Simba only had high latency but no loss, it tolerated it ("weighing the RTT difference") — exactly the sensible trade-off our reward implies. When the link recovered, it returned to normal. Reaction time: 1–2 seconds. Every move came with a readable reason.

The takeaway for the paper

We now have a live, explainable edge controller that changes the application-layer bonding policy within seconds in response to realistic per-SIM degradation — and, as a bonus, the experiment itself exposed that transport-layer MPTCP and our application-layer AI are two distinct controllers that both react to the same impairment.
